Professional Self-Assessment

Before studying computer science, I worked as a bartender and server, where I learned to stay calm under pressure and solve problems for people in real time. I then completed a full-stack web development bootcamp through the University of Pennsylvania. I enrolled at SNHU to go deeper than web development and earn a bachelor’s degree in computer science, with concentrations in cybersecurity and software engineering. I’m pursuing roles where I can improve systems, find efficiency gains, and build software with security in mind. My degree taught me that security works best as part of the design, not as a layer added afterward. I want to work in security-focused software or security analysis, helping teams build systems that are secure from the start. I also want my work to reflect my values, particularly respect for human rights, dignity, and privacy. That means being thoughtful about the organizations and projects I contribute to, and choosing not to work on systems designed to automate weapons or military targeting.

Strengths and how the program shaped them

My strongest asset is a habit of going a few layers deeper than the task requires. When I started the program, my programming experience was primarily in full-stack web development with JavaScript. Since then, I've worked with C++, Python, and Rust, developing an understanding of how different languages approach programming problems and the trade-offs involved in choosing one over another. I can now evaluate a language based on a project's requirements, including considerations such as compiled versus interpreted execution, performance, and memory safety, rather than defaulting to the language I already know.

The course that changed my understanding most was Software Reverse Engineering. Decoding a compiled binary instruction by instruction showed me how software interacts with the machine, and that knowledge makes higher-level languages easier to reason about. My security coursework deepened that understanding further. Learning how vulnerabilities such as buffer overflows and other memory-safety bugs occur required me to understand not just what the code was doing, but how the language handled memory and where its protections ended. Together, these experiences have changed how I approach software: I look beyond what the code does to understand why it behaves that way, what assumptions it relies on, and where those assumptions can fail.

Collaboration in a team environment

Most of my coursework was completed individually, so much of my collaborative experience comes from outside the classroom. In pastry school, I was paired with a lab partner for practical exams, and we divided each task based on our strengths. For a fresh fruit tart, I made the pastry and arranged the fruit, since careful handling prevents overworking the dough and the arrangement needed a steady, deliberate hand. My partner, who had a culinary background, made the pastry cream and apricot glaze because stovetop work played to his experience. Our tart was one of twenty chosen to be served at a special dinner. That experience taught me to assess a task before dividing it, to assign work based on each person’s strengths, and to rely on a partner whose experience was very different from mine. I would apply the same approach to software development by identifying the strengths of each team member, dividing work around clear responsibilities, and coordinating integration so that independently developed components work together.

Communicating with stakeholders

My experience in customer-facing roles taught me that effective communication starts with understanding what the other person needs, rather than simply providing information. I find that when communicating technical concepts to a non-technical audience, it’s helpful to employ analogous terms they are familiar with. For example, when explaining public key cryptography, I have used the analogy of a locked mailbox with a slot. The slot is the same as the public key, anyone can put a message in the mailbox. The private key, like the key to the mailbox is the only way to retrieve, or in this case decrypt, the message. The most important part of communicating in any circumstance is understanding your audience and conveying information in a way that is accessible to them. I see the same principle applying to software development, where stakeholders may understand the problem they want solved without knowing the technical details of the solution.

My coursework at SNHU also helped me become more receptive to feedback. Early on, I focused on making the requested changes. Over time, I learned to use feedback to find gaps in my understanding and improve my next iteration. I want to carry that approach into a professional environment by asking clarifying questions, communicating technical trade-offs in accessible language, and confirming that the finished solution meets the intended requirements.

Data structures and algorithms

My coursework has taught me to approach computational problems by considering how data is represented, how information is processed, and how algorithmic choices affect efficiency and accuracy. I have developed an understanding of data structures such as arrays, vectors, and hash maps, as well as the trade-offs involved in choosing among them. I have also learned to evaluate algorithmic complexity and recognize that the most sophisticated solution is not always the most appropriate one. The right choice depends on the size of the dataset, the operations a program must perform, and the requirements of the application. This perspective has helped me move beyond simply writing code that produces the expected result toward evaluating whether a solution is efficient, maintainable, and appropriate for its intended purpose.

Software engineering and databases

Throughout the program, I have developed a broader understanding of software engineering as a discipline that involves more than implementing individual features. Effective software requires thoughtful architecture, clear separation of responsibilities, appropriate data modeling, validation, testing, and consideration of how a system will evolve over time. My experience working across several programming languages has helped me recognize how language features and design patterns influence maintainability and reliability. My database coursework has also reinforced the importance of organizing data consistently, validating operations, and considering how an application’s data requirements influence its architecture. I now approach development with greater attention to the relationships between components and the long-term consequences of design decisions.

Security

My understanding of security has evolved from viewing it as a specialized concern to recognizing it as a responsibility that extends throughout the software development lifecycle. My coursework has introduced me to vulnerabilities involving memory management, authentication, input validation, and access control, along with the principles used to prevent or mitigate them. In Software Reverse Engineering, decoding a compiled program instruction by instruction showed me how high-level code maps to machine behavior, which is where many vulnerabilities originate. Learning how low-level program behavior can create vulnerabilities has strengthened my appreciation for secure programming practices and the importance of understanding a language’s guarantees and limitations. I have also learned that security depends on more than preventing individual coding errors: it requires considering trust boundaries, limiting privileges, protecting credentials, and evaluating how systems may be misused. I want to apply this perspective to software development by considering security requirements from the beginning and treating testing, risk assessment, and ongoing improvement as essential parts of building reliable systems.

How the artifacts fit together

My three enhancements share one design habit: replacing implicit assumptions with explicit, tested rules. The CMS port (Enhancement One) began with a compiled C++ executable, not its original source code. I reverse engineered the binary, reconstructed its functionality in C++, refactored the recovered code, and then ported the application to Rust. During the reverse-engineering process, I found a single plaintext password shared by every user within minutes of inspecting the executable. The subsequent refactoring and port gave me an opportunity to replace implicit conventions with explicit constraints. Porting it to Rust taught me to replace convention with structure. Customers became structs, service types became an enum that rejects invalid values, and access became role-based. Each user’s password is now stored as an individually salted Argon2 hash. Implementing it taught me how the stored hash string holds its algorithm, parameters, and salt, and how verification recomputes the hash with that same salt. The port also showed a remaining risk: the admin credential is still a hardcoded hash in the source, which I would move to a secrets store in production.

The Grazioso Salvare dashboard (Enhancement Two) taught me that a better algorithm changes what users see. Exact-match queries returned only 4 to 17 candidates per rescue profile. A weighted scoring model (breed 40, sex 30, age 30, with partial credit for age) and a minimum score of 70 surfaced 264, 23, and 26 additional near-matches across the three profiles, and none of the original perfect-score matches were lost. The cutoff was necessary because the charts and map read from the same table, so ranking every animal would have made them describe the whole shelter. The trade-off was time: scoring all records in memory takes 12 to 18 milliseconds at this scale. That was acceptable here, but it’s the kind of cost I’d need to reassess as the dataset grows.

The Grazioso Salvare data layer (Enhancement Three) taught me that validation and permissions protect against different failures. The allow-list stops malicious queries, and the read-only database user limits damage if something gets through. Testing exposed a gap in my first version: a delete filter using $ne could match nearly every record and defeat the empty-filter guard. I fixed it by restricting update and delete to exact-match operators. I discovered that the original connection check did not reliably establish that the database was reachable, because the driver connects lazily. The layer now raises explicit errors and is covered by 23 unit tests.

Together, these enhancements reflect the same principle: making assumptions explicit so they can be examined, enforced, and tested. In the CMS port, the type system and role checks enforce rules that the original program left implicit. In the data layer, the allow-list and database permissions define which operations are permitted and limit the consequences when something goes wrong. In the dashboard, weighted scoring and a defined cutoff make the matching criteria explicit and predictable. Across all three, I learned that reliable software depends not just on making the right assumptions, but on designing systems that can enforce them.
